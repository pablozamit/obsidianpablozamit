
## La ciencia de la curiosidad (Will Storr)

De *The Science of Storytelling*:

- **¿Dónde comienza una historia?** Un nacimiento puede ser el inicio de una vida, pero los datos biográficos brutos tienen poco significado para el cerebro narrador. Muchas historias comienzan con un **momento de cambio inesperado** (clippings #5849-5850):

> "Change is endlessly fascinating to brains... Unexpected change makes us curious, and curious is how we should feel in the opening movements of an effective story."
> _—Will Storr_ (clippings #5851-5852)

- Las aperturas crean curiosidad describiendo momentos concretos de cambio, tan importantes que se comprimen en las primeras frases (clippings #5853-5854).
- **La brecha de información**: los cerebros se vuelven espontáneamente curiosos ante un conjunto de información incompleto:

> "'There is a natural inclination to resolve information gaps,' wrote Loewenstein, 'even for questions of no importance.'"
> _—Will Storr, citando a Loewenstein_ (clipping #5858)

- **Zona de máxima curiosidad**: cuando la gente cree tener alguna idea pero no está del todo segura — ahí es donde juegan los narradores (clipping #5859). Anhelamos saber la respuesta como anhelamos drogas, sexo o chocolate: ese estado placenteramente desagradable ante la promesa deliciosa de una respuesta (clipping #5860); la compulsión era tan grande que la gente pagaba por la información (clipping #5861).
- **Las cuatro formas de inducir curiosidad involuntaria** (Loewenstein): (1) plantear una pregunta o puzzle; (2) secuencia de eventos con resolución esperada pero desconocida; (3) violación de expectativas que dispara la búsqueda de explicación; (4) saber que otra persona posee la información (clipping #5862).

## El cerebro como máquina de predicciones

> "You walk into a room. Your brain predicts what the scene should look and sound and feel like, then it generates a hallucination based on these predictions. It's this hallucination that you experience as the world around you."
> _—Will Storr_ (clipping #5864)

Nunca experimentarás la realidad verdadera porque no tienes acceso directo a ella (clipping #5865).

## Técnicas concretas de escritura vívida

- **Orden de las palabras**: la construcción transitiva ("Jane gave a kitten to her Dad") es más eficaz que la ditransitiva ("Jane gave her Dad a kitten") — imaginar primero Jane, luego el gatito, luego el padre imita la acción real (clipping #5866).
- **Tres cualidades específicas** por objeto para escenas vívidas: "una alfombra azul oscura", "un lápiz naranja a rayas" (clipping #5869).
- **Mostrar, no etiquetar**:

> "'instead of telling us a thing was "terrible"', describe it so that we'll be terrified. Don't say it was 'delightful'; make us say 'delightful' when we've read the description.' The abstract information contained in adjectives such as 'terrible' and 'delightful' is thin gruel for the model-building brain."
> _—Will Storr_ (clipping #5871)

- Evocar los sentidos (toques, sabores, olores, sonidos) recrea mundos en el cerebro del lector (clipping #5872).
- "Nuestra especie ha conquistado la Tierra gracias a nuestra capacidad de entender las mentes de los demás" (clipping #5873).

## Notas sueltas

Picture this scene:

A group of corrupt cops have arrived at a house to arrest a gang leader who is on the run.

They’re putting on their bulletproof vests, loading up their weapons and going over the strategy for the arrest.

Just as they approach the house, the leader says to his crew “Option one is to bring him in alive. But don’t forget there’s always option two”.

==

Did you see the device that’s being used?

I call it stoking the viewer’s imagination…..

By not explicitly stating what option two…. You can’t help but imagine that what the cop is saying to his men is that it’s okay to kill the bad guy.

Which makes you even more eager to keep watching the scene play out.

In [[Copywriting]], there’s a similar device called the slippery slope effect and it’s the best way to learn how to write powerful hooks.

The slippery slope effect states that your entire goal with the first two or three sentences of copy is to get the reader to keep reading….

And that those sentences should be so easy to read that the reader feels as if he’s gently going down a slippery slope.

So that’s the tip. Stoking the reader’s imagination so they feel like they’re going down a slippery slide.

If you can keep this in mind the next time you write and edit a hook… If you can stoke your reader’s imagination by giving them a puzzle to solve instead of solving the puzzle for them….
